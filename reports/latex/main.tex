% ------------------------------------------------------------------ abstract
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A retailer must decide every day, for every product, how much to order and from
which supplier. We build a complete decision-support system for this problem
and evaluate it against tuned classical policies. A simulator calibrated on
1,067,371 real sales records provides the environment. A MaskablePPO agent
chooses quantity and supplier jointly. Four textbook policies serve as
baselines, each tuned on separate seeds. A language model explains each
decision in business English and writes supply-chain crises for stress
testing. All results are scored on 30 held-out seeds with paired tests.

The tuned agent earns \Rs 79.7\lakh{} per 180-day run, which is 90.8\% of the
best classical policy (\Rs 87.8\lakh{}) and above two of the four classical
policies. The remaining gap is statistically significant (paired $t=-6.45$,
$n=30$, 95\% CI [5.5, 10.6]\lakh{}). We trace it to supplier fragmentation:
the agent pays about twice the ordering fees, because its observation
contains no ordering history. Action masking improves the agent roughly
threefold, and hyperparameter tuning closes 38\% of what remains. Training
on LLM-generated crises does \emph{not} improve robustness (paired
$t=+1.69$, not significant), whereas plain tuning does. For the explainer,
5 of 30 raw LLM outputs contained a number the simulator never produced; a
grounding check caught all 5, so delivered accuracy is 100\% against 83\% raw.
We report these negative results as findings, because every comparison was
designed so that a positive result would have meant something.
\end{abstract}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 1 intro
\section{Introduction}

Retail replenishment can fail in two directions that cost different amounts.
Ordering too much ties up money, fills a shared warehouse, and leaves seasonal
goods unsold. Ordering too little loses the sale for good. Supplier choice
adds a second decision: one supplier is cheap but slow, one is fast but dear,
and one looks reasonable but ships only 80\% of an order and goes offline most
often. Because today's order becomes next week's stock, the task is a
sequential decision problem.

Classical rules such as $(s,S)$, EOQ with a reorder point, the newsvendor
model, and forecast-plus-safety-stock treat each product on its own and
assume well-behaved demand. In our data, daily demand has a variance about
216 times its mean, far beyond what a Poisson model allows. Reinforcement
learning (RL) can in principle learn a joint ordering and sourcing policy
that a formula cannot express, and deep RL has been tried on inventory
problems with mixed results against tuned heuristics \cite{gijsbrechts,beer}. A second
obstacle is trust: a buyer will not act on a recommendation they cannot
interpret.

This paper therefore tests a hypothesis rather than assuming it: that an RL
agent can match or beat tuned classical policies, and that an LLM can make its
decisions understandable without making things up. We keep the baselines
strong, keep the test seeds separate, and report the result whichever way it
falls. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An end-to-end system: a calibrated simulator, a MaskablePPO agent
    with a joint quantity--supplier action, four tuned classical baselines,
    an LLM explainer, an LLM crisis generator, and a recommend-only dashboard
    (Sections 3--5).
  \item A fair comparison on 30 held-out seeds with paired significance
    tests, showing that the tuned agent reaches 90.8\% of the best classical
    policy and that the remaining \Rs 8.1\lakh{} gap is real and traceable to
    a specific cause (Sections 6.1 and 7).
  \item Two controlled ablations: action masking is worth about $3\times$ and
    tuning closes 38\% of the rest, while training on LLM-generated crises
    does not improve robustness (Sections 6.2 and 6.3).
  \item A measurement of the explainer showing that the grounding check is
    essential: raw accuracy is 83\%, delivered accuracy 100\% (Section 6.4).
  \item A test of whether the demand pattern is stable across the two years
    of data, answering the concern that the data is old (Section 6.5).
\end{enumerate}

% --------------------------------------------------------------- 2 related
\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Deep RL for inventory.}
Oroojlooyjadid et al.\ \cite{beer} apply a deep Q-network to the beer game and report
performance at or above base-stock policies under cooperation, in a serial
chain with a single product and no sourcing choice. Gijsbrechts et al.\ \cite{gijsbrechts}
apply an actor-critic method to lost-sales and dual-sourcing problems and find
it competitive with tuned heuristics but not clearly superior, at a high
tuning cost. Boute et al.\ \cite{boute} survey the area and name fair benchmarking and
interpretability as open problems. Our setting adds joint quantity and
supplier selection under one shared warehouse, and we treat fair benchmarking
as a design requirement.

\paragraph{Policy optimization and action masking.}
Proximal Policy Optimization \cite{ppo} optimizes a clipped surrogate objective and
is a stable on-policy default. Huang and Ontañón \cite{masking} show that invalid-action
masking is a valid policy gradient and scales to large action spaces, which
is what makes our $21^{10}\approx 1.7\times10^{13}$-option daily action
tractable. We use the Stable-Baselines3 ecosystem \cite{sb3}.

\paragraph{Retail demand data.}
Chen et al.\ \cite{chen} study data mining on online-retail transactions, the same
family of data as the UCI Online Retail II set \cite{uci} that we calibrate on.

\paragraph{Explanation.}
Learned policies output a number, not a reason, so they rarely reach a
decision-maker. We do not claim to open the network. Instead the LLM only
restates facts the simulator computed, and a check rejects any number it
invents (Section 4.4). To our knowledge the combination of an RL inventory
agent, a number-checked LLM explainer, and an LLM that generates the
scenarios used to test it has not been evaluated together; we make no claim
beyond this single setting.

% ------------------------------------------------------------- 3 task setup
\section{Problem Setup}

\paragraph{Data.}
UCI Online Retail II \cite{uci} holds 1,067,371 sales records from a UK online gift
shop, December 2009 to December 2011. Cancelled orders, returns, negative
quantities, and missing product codes are removed, and sales are aggregated to
one row per product per day. Saturdays are dropped because the shop never
traded on them; filling them with zeros would teach a false weekly dip. We
select ten products on purpose to span three demand types: four steady
(coefficient of variation 1.02--1.20), three moderate (1.29--1.65), and three
bursty (2.09--2.34). Only data before 1 June 2011 builds the simulator. The
agent never sees the records: we measure mean demand, variability, weekday
pattern, month pattern, and zero-sales frequency, and the simulator
reproduces them with a Gamma--Poisson (negative binomial) demand model.

\paragraph{A rejected alternative.}
A newer Kaggle retail-inventory dataset (2022--2024) has no weekday and no
seasonal pattern and is synthetic; we rejected it. Newer but synthetic is
worse than older but real for calibrating demand shape.

\paragraph{Environment.}
The environment is a Gymnasium simulator of 10 products, 3 suppliers, and
180 days per run, with a 14-day warm-up that is not scored. All products share
one warehouse of 20,000 units; deliveries beyond capacity are refused and
written off. Each day proceeds in order: deliveries arrive; demand is drawn and
adjusted for weekday and month; sales are made, with any shortfall a lost sale
carried as backlog and penalized; orders are placed (after demand, as a real
buyer orders once the day is known); profit is counted. Leftover stock at the
end is valued at 60\% of cost, and storage costs 0.15\% of item cost per day.
The suppliers (Table~\ref{tab:suppliers}) are invented, since no public
dataset records supplier terms; none dominates.

\paragraph{Decision and objective.}
Each day, for each product, one of $7\times3=21$ options is chosen: a quantity
level and a supplier. Over 10 products this gives $21^{10}\approx1.7\times10^{13}$
daily combinations. The goal is to maximize profit,
\begin{equation}
\text{profit}=\text{sales}-\text{purchases}-\text{storage}-\text{fees}-\text{stockout}-\text{overflow}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Splits.}
Three disjoint seed groups are used: seeds 100--111 tune the classical
policies, seeds 900--905 select checkpoints during training, and seeds
500--529 are used only for reporting. Every method faces the same customers and
delivery delays on each reporting seed, so comparisons are paired.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
There is no ground-truth label in profit optimization, so classification
accuracy is not defined. We report: (i) mean profit per 180-day run;
(ii) relative profit, the agent's profit divided by the best classical profit;
(iii) fill rate, the share of demand served; (iv) ordering, stockout, and
overflow costs; and, for the explainer, (v) \emph{raw accuracy} (grounded and
complete) and \emph{delivered accuracy} (after the check).

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 4 method
\section{Method}

\subsection{System Pipeline}

The system has five parts: the simulator; a MaskablePPO agent; four tuned
classical policies; an LLM explainer; and an LLM crisis generator. A Streamlit
dashboard presents them in shadow mode: the best classical policy is the
\emph{engine}, the agent runs alongside as a \emph{challenger} that is scored
but never acts, and every decision can be explained. The dashboard recommends
and never places an order.

\subsection{Agent and Objective}

The observation has 88 numbers: for each product, stock on hand, stock on
order, days of cover, the 7-day mean demand, demand variability, trend, and
backlog; plus weekday, time of year, and each supplier's availability, lead
time, and recent reliability. The action is a \texttt{MultiDiscrete} vector of
ten entries over 21 joint options. Quantity and supplier are one joint choice,
not two, which trains $2.8\times$ faster and reflects that ordering a large
quantity is only sensible from a supplier able to deliver it. The reward is the
day's profit, scaled and clipped, and zero during warm-up.

We use MaskablePPO. PPO maximizes the clipped surrogate
\begin{equation}
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\!\left[\min\!\big(\rho_t\hat A_t,\;
\text{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\big)\right],
\end{equation}
with $\rho_t=\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t|s_t)$. Masking
adds a vector $m$ to the logits $z$ before the softmax,
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\operatorname{softmax}(z+m)_a,\quad
m_a=\begin{cases}0&\text{legal}\\-\infty&\text{illegal,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
so offline suppliers and orders too large for the warehouse are never sampled.
The network is small (two layers of 128 or 256 units) and trains on CPU.

\subsection{Classical Baselines}

Four textbook policies are implemented over the same 21 options per product as
the agent, so none has finer control than another: $(s,S)$, EOQ with a reorder
point, the newsvendor model, and forecast plus safety stock. Each is tuned by
grid search on the 12 tuning seeds. Tuning matters: widening the search lifted
the best policy from \Rs 69.7\lakh{} to \Rs 89.2\lakh{} on the tuning seeds,
and an untuned baseline would have made the agent look better than it is.

\subsection{LLM Explainer with a Grounding Check}

The LLM is \emph{never asked why} a decision was made. The agent's real reason
is a neural network, and any ``because'' the LLM wrote would be a plausible
story rather than the cause. The LLM instead receives a set of facts $F$
computed by the simulator (stock, orders in transit, days of cover, recent
demand, what was ordered and from whom, that supplier's lead time and
reliability) and writes two or three sentences. Let $N(x)$ denote the set of
numbers appearing in text $x$. The output $y$ is accepted only if
$N(y)\subseteq N(F)$ and its required fields are present; otherwise a
deterministic template is used. Three free models are tried in order
(\texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}, \texttt{glm-5.2}, \texttt{nemotron-3-ultra-550b-a55b})
through OpenRouter, with retry and back-off because free endpoints rate-limit.

\subsection{Crisis Generator and Curriculum}

The LLM is asked for realistic supply-chain crises and returns scenarios such
as a demand spike on four products, a supplier outage, a lead-time delay, and a
cost surge, which overlap in time. Nothing it writes is trusted: the scenario
type must be known, supplier and product indices must exist (out-of-range
product numbers are dropped, not wrapped), values are clamped to safe ranges,
and windows are moved inside the 180-day run. Six deliberately malformed inputs
were all corrected or rejected.

For curriculum training, 16 crisis sets built from 19 scenarios were saved and
split 10/6. The agent trains on a random training set at the start of each
run and is tested on the 6 it never saw. Its seed, settings, and training
length match the untuned agent, so crisis exposure is the only difference.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Main Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:policies} compare all methods on the
30 held-out seeds. The best classical policy, forecast plus safety stock,
earns \Rs 87.8\lakh{}. The tuned agent earns \Rs 79.7\lakh{}, which is
\textbf{90.8\%} of it. The difference is \Rs 8.1\lakh{} (paired
$t=-6.45$, $n=30$, $p<0.001$, 95\% CI [5.5, 10.6]\lakh{}); the classical
policy wins on 26 of 30 seeds. The agent beats two of the four classical
policies, EOQ and $(s,S)$. Against a fixed daily order, which is what a shop
does without any system, the engine raises profit from \Rs 53.3\lakh{} to
\Rs 87.8\lakh{} (+65\%) and wins on all 30 seeds; the agent also beats it on
29 of 30.

\subsection{Is the Data Too Old?}

The simulator uses the \emph{shape} of demand, not prices or volumes, so the
relevant question is whether that shape is a feature of retail or of one
period. Splitting the two years into two halves and measuring each
(Figure~\ref{fig:stability}), the weekday pattern matches strongly
($r=0.87$) and November is the peak month in both. The month pattern is weaker
($r=0.51$, or $0.64$ excluding December, which is partly a data-window
artifact), and burstiness (variance over mean) is extreme in both years (224
and 474). This supports the two features the simulator relies on most. It
cannot speak to demand today, which stays a limitation (Section 9).

\section{Error Analysis}

Where does the \Rs 8.1\lakh{} gap come from? Figure~\ref{fig:trace} shows one
traced run; the aggregate numbers in Table~\ref{tab:main} agree.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ordering fees.} The agent pays \Rs 5.8\lakh{} against
    \Rs 2.9\lakh{}. It spreads orders across all three suppliers: in the
    traced run it placed 719 orders, the classical policy 323, almost all with
    the cheap supplier.
  \item \textbf{Fill rate.} 95.1\% against 97.9\%; the agent runs out more
    often, costing about \Rs 3\lakh{} more in stockout penalties.
  \item \textbf{Overflow.} The agents are the only methods with zero overflow
    loss: they learned the shared-warehouse rule. Every classical policy
    wastes some.
\end{itemize}

The cause of the fee gap is an observability gap. The 88 inputs contain nothing
about the agent's own ordering history: no days since the last order, no
running total of fees, no record of the last supplier used. It pays double and
cannot see that it does. This is a limit on what the agent observes, not a
tuning problem, and adding a few history features per product is the most
promising untried change; we have not tested it.

\section{Efficiency and Deployment}

Everything trains on commodity hardware. A 1M-step run takes about 48 minutes
on a laptop CPU; the 2M-step runs took about 4.8 hours on Kaggle.
Curriculum training is roughly twice as expensive per step as calm training
(measured at 156 steps per second). We did not measure inference latency or
memory.

The result argues for a shadow-mode deployment, which the dashboard
implements: the tuned classical policy is the engine a buyer would run, the
agent runs beside it, logged and scored but not acting, and the explainer
covers whichever decision is shown. When the challenger beats the engine
consistently on live data it can be promoted. Still missing for real use:
handling new products with no history, order-value limits, an audit trail,
a live data feed, and a signal that a human should decide when the system is
unsure.

% --------------------------------------------------------- 9 limitations
\section{Limitations}

We list these in descending order of how much they should change a reader's
confidence.

\paragraph{Data age.}
The data is UK retail from 2009--2011. We showed the demand shape is stable
across the two years we have, but nothing here speaks to current demand, and
recent product-level retail data is not public.

\paragraph{Invented suppliers.}
Lead times, reliability, and fees are hand-set to span the standard trade-off.
No public dataset records them, so absolute rupee values are not forecasts.

\paragraph{Unequal difficulty in curriculum training.}
The crisis-trained agent faced a harder task with the same step budget, so part
of its weaker result may be under-training rather than the method failing.

\paragraph{Explainer sample.}
30 decisions, one method, one seed. The explainer is a checked restatement of
computed facts, not an account of the network's reasoning, and we do not claim
otherwise.

\paragraph{One shop, ten products.}
Other categories, larger assortments, and other cost structures are untested.

\paragraph{Free LLM endpoints.}
Free models rate-limit; explanations can take up to a minute and sometimes fall
back to the template.

% -------------------------------------------------------- 10 broader impact
\section{Broader Impact}

The agent should not be deployed today: it earns less than a simpler method and
is not more robust. The honest use is as a scored challenger. Two risks deserve
naming. An explanation that reads smoothly can lend unwarranted confidence to a
wrong decision, which is why the numbers are checked and why the explanation
is described as a restatement rather than a reason. And because suppliers are
invented and the data is old, rupee figures should be read as relative
comparisons inside one simulator.

% ------------------------------------------------------------ 11 conclusion
\section{Conclusion}

A MaskablePPO agent reaches 90.8\% of the profit of a tuned classical
policy on 30 held-out seeds, beats two of four classical policies, and beats a
fixed daily order on 29 of 30 seeds, but the best classical policy still wins
by \Rs 8.1\lakh{} per run ($t=-6.45$). The result is a negative one for the
hypothesis that RL adapts better than a tuned formula: it did not, in calm
conditions or in crisis. We think the part worth keeping is how the result was
established. The baselines were tuned, the test seeds were separate, the
crisis-trained agent was tested on unseen crises, and the percentage drop was
shown beside absolute profit. Masking and tuning each had a measurable effect;
crisis training did not. The explainer is accurate only because it is
checked: 83\% raw, 100\% delivered.

The clearest next step is indicated by the error analysis. The gap is fees from
supplier fragmentation, and the agent cannot see its own ordering history, so
giving it that history while keeping the same evaluation is the experiment
most likely to change the headline result.
